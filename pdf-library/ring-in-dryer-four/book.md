Chapter 1: Nine Tuesdays

The Spin Room opened at seven, but Nadia got there at half past six, because the tide came over the slipway at twenty to seven and after that the buses filled up with people carrying buckets and nets and damp dogs. She liked the laundromat before the buckets. She liked the smell of it first thing: washing powder, hot lint, the faint burnt-sugar smell of the tumble dryers, and underneath all of it the sea, which got into everything on Pell Street eventually.

There were six washers and four dryers. Dryer four had been out of order since March, when the drum belt snapped and Aunt Priya decided it was cheaper to hang a laminated sign on it than to call the man from Bexhill. The sign said SORRY — OUT OF ORDER in letters that had faded from red to pink. People still opened it, out of habit or hope, and closed it again.

Nadia worked Tuesdays and Thursdays. On Tuesdays she also worked the quiet hour before the shop opened, when she swept the floor and emptied the lint traps and wrote up the ledger. The ledger was Aunt Priya's idea: a hardback notebook, ruled, in which every machine was listed down the left side and every hour across the top. If a customer's wash went missing, or a sock turned up three days later behind a drum, you could look at the ledger and say, with some authority, that on Tuesday at eleven Mrs. Okafor had used washer three and nothing had been found in it but a hairgrip.

Nadia had been keeping the ledger for two years. She had also been painting in the back room for two years, where the old boiler used to be, and she had deferred her place at art college twice. The first time it was money. The second time it was Aunt Priya's hip, which had been replaced in June and which was, everyone agreed, doing very well, thank you, though Priya still took the stairs one at a time and swore at the last step in Gujarati.

On the ninth Tuesday, the ring was in the lint trap of dryer four.

Nadia found it at ten past seven, when she opened the trap to check it, as she did every Tuesday, even though the machine had not turned since March. The trap was a grey plastic drawer, and in it, among the grey fluff, sat a ring: brass, thin, with a green glass stone the size of a pea.

She had found eight others. They were in a Fox's biscuit tin under the counter, next to the spare fuses. Each one was the same: brass band, green stone, a seam at the back where the metal had been joined, and inside the band, in tiny raised letters, the words MADE IN TAIWAN.

Nadia stood in the empty laundromat with the ring in her palm and listened to the sea, which was doing its morning thing, dragging shingle. Then she put the ring in the tin, wrote the date in the ledger in the margin beside dryer four — 9th ring, 7:10 — and went to unlock the front door.

Aunt Priya arrived at nine, as she did on Tuesdays, with two coffees and a paper bag of samosas that she claimed were for Nadia and then ate one herself. She had her hair in a scarf and her reading glasses on a chain, and she walked as if her new hip were a rumour she had decided to ignore.

"Any trouble?" she said.

"Dryer four gave me another ring."

Aunt Priya put the samosas down. "Show me."

Nadia fetched the tin. Aunt Priya opened it, looked at the rings for a long moment, and closed it again.

"Throw them out," she said.

"You said that last time."

"And you didn't."

"I want to know who's doing it."

Aunt Priya took her coffee to the counter and sat on the high stool, the one with the split seat that she refused to replace. "It's a joke," she said. "Some kid. Some kid thinks it's funny."

"A kid who comes at seven in the morning, nine weeks running, and puts a ring in a broken dryer?"

"Kids are strange."

"Not that strange."

Aunt Priya looked at the front window, where the sea showed as a grey stripe between the chip shop and the charity shop. "Leave it, Nadia. It's a ring. It's a cheap ring. Nobody's missing it."

"Somebody's missing it enough to leave it here."

"That's not logic, that's poetry," Aunt Priya said, which was what she always said when Nadia was right and she did not want to admit it. Then she ate the second samosa and asked Nadia to put the new timetable in the window.

Chapter 2: The Back Vent

The first thing Nadia did was read the ledger backwards. It took her an hour, because the ledger was not designed for reading backwards, and because she kept stopping to check that Mrs. Okafor really had used washer three at eleven on the eleventh of March, which she had.

Nobody had used dryer four since the seventh of March, when the drum belt snapped in the middle of a load of beach towels and the machine made a noise like a dropped spanner and stopped. Aunt Priya had refunded the customer, put the sign on the door, and that was that. The machine was still plugged in. Its door still opened. Its drum still turned if you pushed it with your hand, half a revolution, then stuck.

So the rings were not coming through the machine. They were being put into it, or into its lint trap, from somewhere else.

The lint trap sat at the bottom of the front panel, a drawer you pulled out. To reach it, you had to be inside the laundromat. Unless.

Nadia knelt behind dryer four with a torch. The back of the machine was a sheet of painted metal with a vent grille in the lower left corner, and the grille was held on by four screws, and two of the screws were missing. She put her finger through the gap and felt cold air, and beyond the cold air, the rough brick of the alley wall.

The alley ran behind the shops from Pell Street down to the boat yard. It was four feet wide, full of bins and gulls and the kind of puddles that never quite dried. The back door of the laundromat opened onto it, and so did the back door of the Marina Fish Bar, and the back door of the charity shop, which was painted blue and had a sign on it that said DELIVERIES ONLY, PLEASE KNOCK LOUDLY.

Nadia went out into the alley. It was eight in the morning and the gulls were already arguing over something in a bin bag. She crouched by the vent. On the ground, in the grit, there was a mark: a clean patch the size of a saucer, where something had been set down and then picked up. She put her hand flat beside it. The patch was dry. Everything else was wet with the morning.

She looked at the vent from the outside. The grille was loose; a person could hook two fingers through it and pull, and the whole thing would come away. Behind it, a foot of dark space, and then the back of the lint trap.

So that was the mechanism. Someone came down the alley, pulled the vent, and posted a ring through the gap. It would fall into the trap, or beside it, and in the morning Nadia would open the drawer and find it sitting in the fluff like an egg in a nest.

She sat back on her heels. The alley smelled of old frying oil and seaweed. Above her, the charity shop's back window was open, and someone inside was playing a radio phone-in about potholes.

There was one more thing. On the brick below the vent, at about the height of a child's shoulder, there was a smudge. Nadia touched it. It was tacky, and it smelled of vinegar.

She went back inside and wrote in the ledger, in the margin, in her small square handwriting: Vent loose. Vinegar. Someone small.

Then she did the washing-up from yesterday, because the machines did not care about mysteries.

The pier arcade opened at ten. Nadia went on her lunch break, which she was entitled to and almost never took. The arcade was a long shed with a carpet the colour of a bruised plum and machines that flashed and chirped and gave nothing away. At the back, between a claw machine full of plush crabs and a penny waterfall, there was a vending machine with a glass dome. Inside the dome, in a slow rotating heap, were rings. Brass rings with green glass stones. Fifty pence a go.

Nadia put in fifty pence. The machine thought about it, then dropped a plastic capsule into the tray. She opened it. The ring inside was identical to the nine in the biscuit tin, down to the seam at the back and the tiny raised letters.

She stood there with the ring in her hand while the penny waterfall clattered and a child on the claw machine lifted a plush crab six inches and dropped it again.

Fifty pence. Nine weeks. Four pounds fifty, if you counted the one in her hand.

Somebody was spending fifty pence a week to put a ring in a broken dryer, and had been doing it since the middle of September.

Chapter 3: Chips at Six

Nadia told Aunt Priya she was staying late to finish a canvas, which was true in the way that a lot of things Nadia said were true: technically accurate and not the whole story. She had a canvas in the back room, a big one, a painting of the laundromat at closing time with all the machines dark and the window full of sea. She had been working on it for four months. The trouble was the light in the window. She could not get the sea to look like the sea and not like a blue blanket.

She stayed on Tuesday night. The back room had a camp bed in it, a kettle, and a radio, and a smell of turpentine that she had stopped noticing years ago. She set her alarm for five thirty, made tea, and sat in the dark by the vent with the torch off and her back against dryer four.

The laundromat at night was a different building. The machines ticked as they cooled. The fridge hummed. The window showed nothing but the street lamp and, beyond it, a blackness that was the sea.

At six exactly, she heard footsteps in the alley. Not adult footsteps. A quick, light, uneven walk, the walk of someone who is trying to be quiet and not quite managing it. Then a scrape, metal on brick. Then a small clink.

Nadia counted to five, then stood up and opened the back door.

The boy was halfway down the alley, a dark shape against the grey. He stopped when the door opened. He was wearing a school jumper that was too big for him, the sleeves pulled over his hands, and he was holding a paper cone of chips in one hand and something small and bright in the other.

"Hey," Nadia said. "Wait."

He ran.

She could have caught him. He was twelve, maybe thirteen, and the alley was full of bins. But she was barefoot, and it was six in the morning, and something in the way he ran — arms tight, head down, like a boy carrying something breakable — made her not want to.

She stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame. The alley was a long grey throat between two walls, and the bins made humps in the dark, and somewhere near the boat yard a gate was banging in the wind, on and on, patient as a clock. Her feet were cold on the concrete. A gull walked past the doorway, looked at her, and went on walking, as if it had seen everything the town did at six in the morning and had stopped being impressed.

She thought about following him. She thought about the way he had said nothing, not one word, and about the chip he had dropped, and about how a person learns to run like that.

She stood in the doorway and watched him go. At the end of the alley he turned left, towards the boat yard, and the gulls lifted off the bins around him like a bad-tempered crowd.

On the ground, where he had been standing, there was a chip. One chip, fat and pale, gone cold. Beside it, a paper receipt, curled at the edges and damp from the ground. Nadia picked it up and smoothed it against her palm.

MARINA FISH BAR. 2 HADDOCK AND CHIPS. 1 SAUSAGE. 6:02. £9.40. CARD.

Two haddock. One sausage. A boy alone at six in the morning does not order two haddock and a sausage. A boy at six in the morning orders chips, if he orders anything, because chips are the cheapest thing that will fill you up.

Unless he was not alone. Unless someone bought them for him, or he was bringing them home, or he was buying them for someone who was waiting.

Nadia put the receipt in the biscuit tin with the rings. Then she went back inside and lay on the camp bed and looked at the ceiling, where the damp had made a shape like a dog's head, and thought about the smell of vinegar on the brick, and the boy's too-big jumper, and the way he had run.

At seven she opened the shop. At ten past seven she opened the lint trap of dryer four and found nothing there, which was, in its way, the most interesting thing that had happened all week.

Chapter 4: Alfie

The Marina Fish Bar opened at eleven thirty, but Sal was in the back from nine, filleting. Nadia found him there with the radio on and a bucket of ice at his feet, and he listened to her question with the flat, patient expression of a man who had been asked a great many strange things by a great many people and had learned to wait for the useful part.

"Little lad," he said. "School jumper, bit big on him. Comes Tuesdays. Sometimes Fridays."

"What does he buy?"

"Two haddock, one sausage, chips. Pays with a card. Always the same card, always the same order." Sal wiped his knife on a cloth. "He waits for it. Sits on the bench outside with the bag on his knees. I asked him once if it was for a party. He said no. He said it was for later."

"For later?"

"That's what he said." Sal shrugged. "I thought, maybe his mum's on shifts. You get a lot of that round here. Nurses, cleaners, the hotels. Somebody's always coming off a shift at six in the morning."

"Does he come with anyone?"

"Never seen anyone with him." Sal looked at her. "Why? He in trouble?"

"No," Nadia said. "I don't think so."

"Good," Sal said, and went back to the fish. "He's a polite kid. Says thank you. You don't get that much now."

The caravan park sat behind the boat yard, behind a fence of wire and a sign that said PRIVATE — RESIDENTS AND VISITORS ONLY, and below that, in older paint, SEA VIEWS. There were no sea views. There was a hedge, a row of static caravans with green gas bottles, and a washing line with two shirts on it, going stiff in the wind.

Nadia found the right caravan because there was a bicycle leaning against the step, a small BMX with a bent spoke and a strip of tape round the handlebar. The door was open. Inside, a radio was playing and someone was singing along, badly, in the way of a person who has been alone in a small space for a long time.

She knocked. The singing stopped.

Frank Nunn was seventy-odd, with the arms of a man who had spent his life lifting engines, and a face that had been out in the weather so long it had gone the colour and texture of a rope. He looked at her, then at the biscuit tin under her arm, and his expression changed in a way she did not expect. It was not suspicion. It was relief.

"You're from the laundrette," he said.

"I'm Nadia. My aunt owns it."

"Priya." He nodded. "I know Priya. She had that hip done."

"She did."

He stood aside and let her in. The caravan was tidy in the way of a place where a man and a boy live without a woman: everything put away, nothing put away properly. There was a photograph on the shelf, a young woman with her arm round a smaller version of Alfie, both of them laughing at something outside the frame.

"Alfie's at school," Frank said. "He'll be back at four. If you've come to tell me he's been nicking, you can save it. He's not a nicking kid."

"He's not nicking," Nadia said. "He's been buying."

She put the tin on the table and opened it. Nine brass rings with green glass stones, and one paper receipt.

Frank looked at them for a long time. Then he sat down, heavily, on the bench seat, and rubbed his face with both hands.

"His mum," he said. "Jo. My daughter."

"Where is she?"

"Cornwall. St Ives. She's got a job in a hotel, housekeeping, and a room in a shared house, and she sends money every month, and she rings on Sundays." He said it like a list he had learned by heart. "She went a year and a half ago. It was meant to be six months. Her mum — my wife — died, and Jo couldn't stay in the town, couldn't walk down Pell Street without seeing her. So she went. And Alfie stayed with me, because the school's here, and his friends are here, and because I said I'd look after him."

"And the rings?"

Frank shook his head. "He's been doing something with fifty-pences. I thought it was the machines in the arcade. All kids do that."

"He's been leaving them in dryer four at the laundrette. Every Tuesday, at six in the morning, before school."

Frank's face did something complicated. "Why?"

"Because of a story," Nadia said.

She had worked it out on the walk over, and she told him now, and the telling felt like lifting a stone and finding the whole thing underneath, damp and complete.

Years ago, not long after Priya bought the shop, back when it was still called Sudsy's and the machines were green, Jo Nunn had worked there on Saturdays. She had been nineteen. She had lost a ring in one of the dryers — not a brass ring, a real one, her grandmother's — and the woman who ran the shop had found it in the lint trap and kept it behind the counter in an envelope with Jo's name on it, and when Jo came back a week later, crying, the woman had given it to her and said: nothing that goes into a machine is ever really lost, it just waits in the lint until you come back for it.

Jo had told that story to her son, the way you tell a child a story about yourself when you want him to know that you were once young and careless and lucky.

And Alfie, who was eleven when his mother left and twelve now, had decided that the story worked both ways.

"He thinks if he puts a ring in the machine," Frank said slowly, "she'll come back for it."

"He thinks she'll come back for it," Nadia said. "Yes."

Frank was quiet for a while. The radio played a song about a road. Outside, a gull landed on the caravan roof and walked about, and the sound of its feet was like someone knocking politely and being ignored.

"Two haddock," Frank said at last. "That's her favourite. Jo's. He buys her tea and brings it home and puts it in the fridge for later."

"Does he eat it?"

"Sometimes. Sometimes it sits there till I throw it out." Frank looked at the rings again. "I should have asked him. I thought he was just — you know. Being a kid."

"He is being a kid," Nadia said. "That's what being a kid is. You believe the story."

Chapter 5: The Letter

Aunt Priya cried in the back room for about a minute and a half, then blew her nose, said something sharp in Gujarati about the damp, and made tea.

"I remember her," she said. "Jo. She had a green coat and she was always late. She used to bring her lunch in a Tupperware and eat it sitting on washer six with her feet up, and I'd tell her to get her feet off the machine, and she'd say, Priya, the machine doesn't mind."

"Did she lose a ring?"

"She lost a ring." Priya stirred the tea. "It was her grandmother's. She was in a state. I found it in the trap of dryer two and I put it in an envelope and I didn't tell her, because I wanted to see her face when I gave it to her. That was a good day. I've had worse."

"So the story's true."

"The story's true. The story's also twenty years old and the shop's changed hands and the machines are different and none of that matters to a twelve-year-old." Priya sat down. "What do we do?"

"We write to her."

"To Cornwall."

"Alfie writes. We post it. He's got a right to tell her what he's been doing. And she's got a right to know her son's been buying her tea every Tuesday for the best part of a year."

Priya looked at the tin. "And the rings?"

"He can decide what to do with them."

They did it on Thursday, after school. Alfie came in with his bag on both shoulders and his hood up, and when he saw Nadia behind the counter he stopped in the doorway like a rabbit that had heard a gate.

"It's all right," Nadia said. "Your grandad knows. Sit down."

He sat. He was a thin boy with a sharp chin and his mother's photograph's eyebrows. He looked at the tin on the counter and did not touch it.

"I'm not in trouble?"

"You're not in trouble." Nadia pushed a sheet of paper across the counter. "You're going to write a letter."

It took an hour. He wrote three drafts. The first was one line: Dear Mum, I am fine. The second was two pages about the football team and a dog that had got into the arcade and would not come out. The third was the one they sent, and it was four lines long, and it said:

Dear Mum,

I have been putting rings in the dryer at the laundrette every Tuesday. They are from the arcade. They cost 50p. I thought if I put enough in, the machine would give one back to you and you would come and get it. I know that is not how it works now. Nadia says nothing that goes in a machine is lost, it just waits. I am not going to do it any more. I am not cross. I have been buying you fish and chips on Tuesdays. Grandad says you like haddock. I hope you are all right.

Love, Alfie

Priya read it, nodded, and added a line at the bottom in her slanting handwriting: Jo — the shop's still here. So am I. Come in for a coffee when you're next down. P.

They posted it on Friday. It went into the letterbox on Pell Street with a Christmas card from Priya's sister and a water bill, and Nadia stood on the pavement afterwards and felt, absurdly, as if she had just put something valuable into a machine and was waiting to see whether it would come back.

That same week, the letter from the art college came.

It had been on the mat on Wednesday, and Priya had put it on the counter, and Nadia had not opened it for two days. She knew what it was. She had deferred twice; the place did not stay open forever. The envelope had the college's name on it in a font that looked like it had been designed by someone who had never had to worry about money.

On Saturday night, Priya found her in the back room, sitting on the camp bed, looking at the envelope.

"Open it," Priya said.

"I know what it says."

"Then it won't surprise you."

Nadia opened it. It said that her place had been held, that the deadline for acceptance was the thirty-first of January, that the portfolio interview could be done by video, that they looked forward to welcoming her.

Priya read it over her shoulder. Then she sat down on the camp bed, which creaked, and said, "You're going."

"I can't. The shop —"

"The shop has been here for forty years and it will be here when you come back. I have a hip made of metal and a boy who can work the till and a man from Bexhill who fixes dryers for a hundred and twenty pounds and a cup of tea." Priya took the letter and folded it. "You are twenty-three years old and you are painting the same window over and over because you are afraid to go and look at a different one. I know what that is. I did it too. I stayed here for eleven years after my husband died because the shop was the only place I knew how to be."

"What happened?"

"Nothing happened. I just got older and the fear got boring." Priya stood up, one hand on the wall. "Send it back. Say yes."

Nadia said yes on Monday. She walked to the postbox with the form in her coat pocket and the wind coming off the sea so hard that she had to lean into it, and she posted it, and then she went to work.

Chapter 6: What the Machine Kept

The reply came on a Tuesday, three weeks later, in an envelope with a Cornish postmark and a stamp showing a lighthouse.

Alfie was at school. Frank brought it in himself, in his coat, with his cap in his hand, and stood in the laundromat while Priya opened it with a knife from the drawer.

There were two letters inside, and a photograph. The photograph was of Jo, standing outside a hotel with a blue door, in a dark uniform, squinting into the sun. She looked older than the girl in the caravan. She looked tired and pleased with herself.

The letter to Priya was short. She was sorry. She had not known about the rings, or the fish, or any of it, and reading it had made her sit down on the stairs of the hotel and cry in front of a guest. She had been ashamed to come back. She had told herself six months and then six months more, and the longer she left it the harder it got, and she had convinced herself that Alfie was better off without her turning up and going again. She was coming down in March, on the coach, for a week. She had booked a room in a bed and breakfast on the seafront. She would like to see him, if he wanted to see her.

The letter to Alfie was longer, and Priya did not read it out. She gave it to him when he came in at four, and he took it into the back room and shut the door, and he was in there for twenty minutes, and when he came out his eyes were red and he was holding the letter in both hands like a certificate.

"She's coming in March," he said.

"She's coming in March," Priya said.

"Can I —" He stopped. "Can I still come in on Tuesdays?"

"You can come in whenever you like," Priya said. "But not at six in the morning. Six in the morning is for gulls and people with no sense."

He laughed. It was the first time Nadia had heard him laugh, and it was a good laugh, a boy's laugh, too loud for the room.

The rings went back into the tin, and the tin went into the drawer behind the counter, next to the spare fuses. Alfie did not want them. He said they were stupid. Nadia said they were not stupid, they were a record, and one day he would want to know how many Tuesdays there had been. Nine, she said. Nine Tuesdays, and one more that she had bought herself and never put in the machine.

The man from Bexhill came in February and fixed dryer four. It cost a hundred and twenty pounds and a cup of tea, and when he had finished he ran an empty cycle to check the drum, and the machine turned and turned and made the low, even, satisfying noise that a working machine makes. Priya stood in front of it with her arms folded and watched it as if it were a horse she had bet on.

They put a load in: beach towels, the ones that had been in the machine when it broke, washed again and again over the months and never quite got rid of the smell of the sea. When the cycle finished, Nadia opened the door and pulled out the lint trap, out of habit, and there was nothing in it but fluff.

In March, Jo came. She came into the laundromat on a Saturday morning with her bag still over her shoulder, and Alfie was there, and Frank was there, and Priya made tea for everyone and pretended not to be watching them. Jo stood by washer six and did not put her feet up on it. She looked at the room as if she were checking that it had stayed where she left it.

Nadia was in the back room, painting. She had started a new canvas in January: the laundromat at closing time, all the machines dark, the window full of sea. The old one was in the corner, unfinished, the sea still looking like a blue blanket.

This time she had got it. The window was a rectangle of grey-green light with a white line across it where the waves were, and the light came into the room and lay on the floor in a shape that was not a blanket but a window, a window with the sea in it.

She heard the bell over the door go, and Alfie laughing, and Priya saying something sharp and fond. She dipped her brush and went on painting. In the drawer behind the counter, in a Fox's biscuit tin beside the spare fuses, ten brass rings with green glass stones waited for whatever came next, which was, as far as she could tell, the whole point of a machine.
