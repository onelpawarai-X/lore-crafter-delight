Chapter 1: The Tuesday Customer

Every Tuesday at half past eight, Mr. Pike came into the Rye Loaf and bought one seeded loaf. He was a tall, stooped man with flour still on his cuffs, though he had not milled anything in twenty years. Elsie's grandmother always had the loaf waiting on the counter, wrapped in brown paper, still warm.

"He likes it warm," Gran said. "A man who worked a mill all his life knows what warm means."

Elsie was thirteen and worked the counter on Tuesdays before school. She liked Mr. Pike because he never asked her what she wanted to be when she grew up. He asked her what she had read that week. If she said nothing, he frowned. If she said a book, he nodded once and paid.

One Tuesday, Mr. Pike did not come.

Elsie watched the door until nine. Gran watched it too, though she pretended to be busy with the till. At half past nine, a woman Elsie did not know came in, carrying an umbrella and a folded note.

"Are you the bakery?" the woman asked. "Mr. Pike's niece. He's had a fall. He's in the home over in Wexley now. He asked me to bring you this."

The note was in Mr. Pike's shaky hand: *One last seeded loaf. For the girl who reads.*

Gran made the loaf herself, and when it came out of the oven she set it on the counter. "Take it over to him," she said. "Wexley's only four miles. Take the bus."

Elsie wrapped the loaf and put it in her bag. On the way out, the bag knocked against the doorframe, and she felt something hard inside the bread. Something that was not bread.

Chapter 2: The Map in the Crumb

She opened the loaf on the bus. Inside, near the center, was a lump of wax paper the size of a walnut. She peeled it back and found a folded square of paper, brown at the edges, with a drawing in faded ink.

It was a map of Ashford-on-Well. There was the bakery, a little square with a chimney. There was the stone bridge. There was the church with its squat tower. And there was the mill, drawn larger than anything else, with three crosses around it.

At the bottom, in the same shaky hand, was a line: *For the town's three hundredth. Three marks, three keys, one stone.*

Elsie read it four times. Then she got off the bus at the next stop and ran back to town.

She found her friend Sam in the market square, eating chips. Sam was fourteen and had a bicycle with a basket. He read the map over her shoulder.

"Three hundredth," he said. "The town's tercentenary is this Saturday. There are banners up."

"So it's a treasure hunt," Elsie said.

"Or a joke," Sam said. "Old men bake strange things."

"Mr. Pike doesn't joke," Elsie said. "He asks what you've read."

They decided to follow the first cross, which sat just beside the stone bridge. Under the bridge, the water was low, and the bank was muddy. They climbed down, getting their shoes wet, and searched the stones.

Sam found it: a tin box wedged into a gap, rusted shut, with a key inside.

The key was small and brass. Tied to it was a paper tag: *For the church.*

Chapter 3: Three Marks

The church was open for the flower committee. They slipped in past the altar and climbed the narrow stairs to the bell chamber. The second cross on the map pointed to the base of the bell rope.

There, tucked behind a loose brick, was a second tin, and inside it a second key, this one iron, with a tag: *For the mill.*

"The mill's been shut for years," Sam said. "The council locked it up."

"Then it's a good thing we have keys," Elsie said.

The mill stood at the end of Mill Lane, a great stone building with a wheel that no longer turned. The door was padlocked, but the padlock was old and the iron key fit it exactly.

Inside, dust hung in the shafts of light from the high windows. The floor was covered in old sacks and broken slats. In the middle of the room stood a millstone, huge and gray, with a hollow carved into its center.

The third cross was drawn right on the millstone.

In the hollow was a wooden box, and in the box were letters. Dozens of them, tied in bundles with string, dated one hundred years ago. There were photographs, too, and a ledger with the names of every family in Ashford-on-Well.

At the top of the bundle was a letter addressed *To whoever finds this, in the year of the three hundredth.*

Elsie read it aloud. It was written by the miller of 1926, Mr. Pike's grandfather. He had asked every family in town to write a letter to the future, and he had sealed them in the millstone, to be opened on the town's three-hundredth birthday.

"He didn't forget," Elsie said. "He's been keeping it safe his whole life."

Chapter 4: The Tercentenary

They carried the box to the town hall. At first the clerk did not believe them. Then he read the first letter, and his hands began to shake.

By Saturday, the whole town knew. The tercentenary fair was held in the market square, with bunting and a brass band and a table full of cakes. In the middle of the square, on a borrowed wheelchair, sat Mr. Pike, his leg in a cast, his niece beside him.

The mayor opened the wooden box in front of everyone. She read one letter—a girl of twelve, in 1926, writing about her hope that the town would still have a library. Then another, from a fisherman, about the sea. Then another, from a baker, about bread.

People cried. People laughed. Elsie's gran had to sit down.

When the last letter was read, Mr. Pike raised one hand. The square went quiet.

"I baked that map into a loaf every Tuesday for six years," he said. "I wanted to see who would follow it. I thought it would be a child. Children still believe a map means something."

He looked at Elsie. "You asked me once why I never asked what you wanted to be. It's because I already knew. You wanted to be someone who follows the map."

Elsie did not know what to say. Sam nudged her. "Say thank you," he whispered.

"Thank you," she said.

Chapter 5: The New Letters

That evening, the town did something the miller of 1926 had not planned. They wrote new letters. Every family in Ashford-on-Well was given a sheet of paper and asked to write to the town's four-hundredth birthday.

Elsie wrote hers at the bakery counter, with flour on her hands. She wrote about the warm loaf, and the brass key, and the way the whole square had gone quiet when the first letter was read. She wrote that she hoped the town still had a library, and a bakery, and someone who asked children what they had read.

Sam wrote a shorter letter. It said: *Keep the mill. Fix the wheel. It's the best building we have.*

They put the letters in a new wooden box, sealed it with wax, and carried it to the mill. The council had promised to repair the roof. For now, the box sat in the hollow of the old millstone, exactly where the first one had been.

Mr. Pike watched from his wheelchair. "You'll need a map," he said.

Elsie looked at him. "Will you bake one?"

He smiled for the first time that Elsie could remember. "I think I might," he said. "But you'll have to come to Wexley for it. And bring a book."

She promised she would. On the bus home, she looked out at the town—the bridge, the church, the mill—and understood that a map was not really about where things were. It was about who would come looking.
