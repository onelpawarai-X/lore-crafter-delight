Chapter 1: The Hill and the Bicycle

Marrow Bay had one hill, and the hill had nine lamps. Every evening at dusk, Mr. Abernathy climbed the hill on a black bicycle so old that the paint had worn down to the metal. He carried a brass pole in a canvas sling across his back, and at each lamp he stopped, stood on the pedals, and touched the wick with a small flame.

Nia had watched him do it since she was six. She lived in the blue house at the bottom of the hill, and from her bedroom window she could see the lamps come on one by one, like someone counting slowly in the dark.

"Nine," she would whisper when the last one lit. Then she would go to sleep.

Mr. Abernathy was not friendly, exactly. He was the kind of old man who answered questions with facts. When Nia asked why he used a bicycle instead of a car, he said, "A car cannot feel the hill." When she asked why he lit the lamps by hand when there were electric ones in the city, he said, "These lamps are older than the city. They have earned their hands."

One October evening, Nia was sitting on her front step when she heard a crash. She ran up the lane and found Mr. Abernathy on the ground beside his bicycle, holding his ankle. The front wheel was bent against a stone.

"Don't touch the bike," he said through his teeth. "Touch my arm."

She helped him sit on the low wall. His ankle was swelling fast, the color of a plum.

"I'll fetch the doctor," she said.

"The doctor is in the next town. And the lamps—" He looked up the hill. The sky was already going gray. "The lamps need lighting before eight. There's a boat due in tonight."

Nia looked at the bicycle. It was taller than she was. "I can't ride that."

"Then walk," he said. "But walk fast."

Chapter 2: A Borrowed Job

The doctor came at seven and wrapped Mr. Abernathy's ankle. "No climbing for a week," she said. "Maybe two."

Mr. Abernathy sat in his kitchen with his foot on a stool and stared at the wall. Nia stood in the doorway, holding a paper bag of groceries the doctor had asked her to fetch.

"The boat," he said at last. "The *Mira* is coming in with the tide. If the hill lamps are dark, they'll miss the channel and run aground on the shoal."

"Can't someone else light them?"

"Who? The whole town uses electric lights now. Nobody remembers how the wicks work." He looked at her. "You've watched me for six years. What did you learn?"

Nia thought. "You turn the little wheel until the wick is high. Then you light it. Then you turn it back down until the flame is the size of a thumb."

Mr. Abernathy was quiet for a moment. "That's right," he said. "That's exactly right."

The bicycle was still bent. Nia and Mr. Abernathy's neighbor, a broad woman named Mrs. Okafor, carried it into the shed. Mrs. Okafor straightened the wheel with a mallet and a block of wood, more or less. "It'll roll," she said. "It won't be pretty."

Nia wheeled the bicycle to the bottom of the hill. It was heavy, and the seat was far too high. She could not sit on it and reach the pedals. So she did the only thing she could think of: she stood on the pedals, one foot at a time, and pushed.

The first hundred yards were awful. The bike wobbled. The brass pole knocked against her back. She fell once, scraped her palm, and got up again.

By the third lamp, she had found a rhythm. Push, coast, stop, light. Push, coast, stop, light. The wick wheel was stiff, and her fingers smelled of paraffin.

At the sixth lamp, she looked down and saw the town below her, small and warm, windows lit. The sea beyond was black. Somewhere out there was the *Mira*.

She kept climbing.

Chapter 3: The Night of the Storm

The storm arrived faster than anyone expected. By the time Nia reached the eighth lamp, the wind was throwing rain sideways and the bicycle was sliding on the wet stones. She had to walk the last stretch, leaning into the weather.

The eighth lamp would not light. The wick was damp. She cupped her hand around it, but the wind kept snatching the flame away. Her matches were almost gone—three left.

She thought of Mr. Abernathy's voice: *Turn the wick high, light it, then bring it down.*

She turned the wheel until the wick stood tall. She struck a match and held it in the shelter of her jacket. The wick caught, flared, and then held, a small orange heart in the glass.

"Eight," she said to the wind.

The ninth lamp was at the very top, on the headland above the bay. The path there was narrow, and the rain had turned it to mud. Nia left the bicycle at the base of the last rise and climbed on foot, the brass pole over her shoulder, the last two matches in her pocket.

At the top, the wind was so loud she could hear nothing else. The lamp stood at the edge of the cliff, its glass streaked with rain. She turned the wick, struck her second-to-last match, and the flame caught.

Then she heard it: an engine, low and steady, coming through the dark.

She looked down at the water. A small boat was moving slowly toward the channel, its single light swinging. The *Mira*.

Nia stood beside the ninth lamp and waved both arms, though she knew they could not see her. The boat turned, just slightly, following the line of lamps down the hill.

Chapter 4: Lamps for the Boat

The *Mira* came in at half past nine. Nia watched from the headland as it slid past the shoal and into the harbor, where a crowd of people with umbrellas were waiting. She could hear the cheers, faint and far away.

She climbed down, collected the bicycle, and walked it home. Her hands were shaking. Her jacket was soaked through. She had never been so tired in her life.

The next morning, Mr. Abernathy was waiting on her front step, his ankle wrapped, a cane beside him. He looked at her for a long moment.

"You lit all nine," he said.

"Eight and nine were hard," Nia said. "The wind."

"The wind is always hard." He nodded slowly. "The *Mira* came in. The captain said he followed the lamps. He didn't know who lit them."

Nia shrugged. "It doesn't matter who."

"It matters to me," Mr. Abernathy said. He reached into his coat and took out a small brass key. "This opens the lamp cases. I'm not going to be able to climb for a while. Maybe not ever, the way this ankle feels."

Nia looked at the key. "You want me to light them?"

"I want you to learn properly," he said. "Not by watching. By doing. Every evening, I'll sit at the bottom of the hill and tell you what you're doing wrong."

Nia took the key. It was warm from his pocket.

"What about the bicycle?" she asked.

"We'll lower the seat," he said. "And maybe paint it. It's been black long enough."

That evening, Nia climbed the hill again. The seat was lower. The bicycle still wobbled, but less. At each lamp she stopped, turned the wick high, lit it, and brought it down to the size of a thumb.

At the top, she looked out at the sea. The *Mira* was tied up in the harbor, its light steady. Below her, the town was lighting its windows one by one.

"Nine," she said.

Then she rode home in the dark, the brass key in her pocket, and for the first time in six years, she did not need to count. She knew.
