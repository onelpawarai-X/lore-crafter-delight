Chapter 1: The Van and the Tuba

The Bindon Silver Band had fourteen members, eleven instruments, and one tuba that had been in the family of Mr. Dunlop for three generations. The tuba was called Doris. Mr. Dunlop spoke to it, polished it on Sundays, and claimed it had a personality. "Doris is a contralto," he said. "She doesn't like the cold."

Two days before the summer fair, a delivery van backed into the band hut while trying to turn around. The driver said he had not seen the hut. The hut was eleven feet tall and painted bright blue, but the driver was from out of town, so the village forgave him, mostly.

Inside, the band's instruments were arranged in a neat row for polishing. The van went through the wall and stopped on top of them. When Mrs. Pratchett arrived, she found the tuba flattened into a shape she later described as "a very sad hat." The trumpets were bent. The clarinet had become a sort of wooden pretzel. The drum had survived, because drums are stubborn.

Mrs. Pratchett was the bandleader. She was seventy-one, had a voice like a foghorn, and had never cancelled anything in her life, including a concert during a hurricane in 1987. She stood in the wreckage with her hands on her hips.

"We are playing on Saturday," she said.

"With what?" asked Mr. Dunlop, holding Doris's remains.

"With whatever we have," said Mrs. Pratchett. "Go home. Bring pots."

Chapter 2: The Quiet Orchestra

The village hall smelled of floor polish and old chairs. On Thursday evening, twenty-three people turned up, most of them not band members, because word had spread that Mrs. Pratchett was planning something.

On the stage was a collection of objects: four kettles, six buckets, a watering can, three wooden spoons, a cheese grater, a bicycle bell, a set of keys, two saucepans, a length of drainpipe, and the surviving drum.

"This," said Mrs. Pratchett, "is the Quiet Orchestra."

"It's not quiet," said Mr. Dunlop. "It's kitchenware."

"Everything is kitchenware if you're brave enough," said Mrs. Pratchett.

She assigned parts. The kettle section—three women from the WI—would provide the melody by tapping the lids with teaspoons. The bucket section, four sturdy men, would supply the bass by hitting the bottoms with their palms. The twins, Maisie and Dot, aged nine, would play the spoons, which they already did at home, to their mother's despair. The vicar was given the bicycle bell and told to ring it "only when moved by the spirit." Mr. Dunlop, who could not bear to be parted from a brass instrument, was given the watering can and instructed to blow across the top.

"It makes a sound like a startled owl," he reported.

"Perfect," said Mrs. Pratchett. "We need an owl."

Chapter 3: Rehearsal in the Village Hall

The first rehearsal was a disaster. The kettles were out of tune with the buckets. The spoons were too fast. The watering can produced a noise that made the vicar's dog howl. The drainpipe, which was meant to be a horn, produced only a low moan, like a cow in a well.

"Stop," said Mrs. Pratchett, after the fourth attempt at "Land of Hope and Glory." "We are thinking like a brass band. We are not a brass band. We are a village."

"What's the difference?" asked Mr. Dunlop.

"A brass band plays the same thing at the same time," said Mrs. Pratchett. "A village plays whatever it can, whenever it can, and somehow it works out."

They tried again. This time, instead of forcing the kettles to follow the buckets, Mrs. Pratchett let each section find its own rhythm. The buckets thumped a steady beat. The kettles tapped a tune over the top. The spoons chattered. The watering can hooted at odd moments. The vicar rang the bell whenever he felt like it, which was often.

It was not "Land of Hope and Glory." It was something else. Something that sounded like a kitchen having a party.

The vicar's dog stopped howling and wagged its tail.

"Again," said Mrs. Pratchett.

They rehearsed until ten o'clock. By the end, the kettles had found a tune, the buckets had found a groove, and Mr. Dunlop had discovered that the watering can, if you blew gently, could produce a note that was almost, almost beautiful.

"I shall call her Doris the Second," he announced.

Chapter 4: The Fair

Saturday was hot and bright. The fair filled the village green with stalls selling jam, raffle tickets, and suspiciously large vegetables. The Quiet Orchestra set up on a platform of hay bales. Mrs. Pratchett wore a hat with a feather. Mr. Dunlop wore a bow tie and held the watering can like a baby.

There was a problem. The repaired instruments, promised by a shop in the next town, had not arrived. A man on a motorbike brought a message: the van with the instruments had broken down, and they would be there by four.

The fair's organizer, a nervous man named Mr. Pettigrew, wrung his hands. "We've advertised a brass band."

"You've got a village," said Mrs. Pratchett. "Sit down."

She raised her teaspoon. The kettles tapped. The buckets thumped. The spoons chattered. The watering can hooted. The vicar rang his bell. The crowd, at first puzzled, began to smile. Then to clap. Then to sing along, badly, which is the best way.

They played for forty minutes. They played "Land of Hope and Glory," "Danny Boy," a medley of sea shanties, and a piece Mrs. Pratchett had written herself, called "The Kettle's Lament." The twins did a spoon solo. Mr. Dunlop's watering can produced one perfect, mournful note that hung over the green like a gull.

At four o'clock, the instruments arrived. The band played them, of course. They were polished and repaired and in tune. But when they finished, the crowd called for the Quiet Orchestra. So they played one more, with kettles and buckets and spoons, and the brass players joined in on the chorus, and nobody minded that it was a little out of tune.

Chapter 5: Encore

The van driver, who had stayed in the village out of guilt, offered to pay for the instruments. Mrs. Pratchett said he could instead join the bucket section. He did. He turned out to have excellent rhythm.

Mr. Dunlop got a new tuba, but he kept the watering can. It sat on his mantelpiece, next to a photograph of Doris the First. "You never forget your first," he said. "Or your second."

The Quiet Orchestra became a regular thing. They played at the harvest supper, the Christmas market, and a wedding where the bride requested the Kettle's Lament. They were never very good. They were never very quiet, either. But whenever the village needed music, they made it out of whatever was in the kitchen.

Years later, someone asked Mrs. Pratchett what the secret was. She thought for a moment.

"Any fool can make music with a trumpet," she said. "It takes a village to make music with a kettle."

Then she raised her teaspoon, and the buckets began to thump.
