Chapter 1: The Shop on Candle Street

Candle Street is short and bends like a comma, as if the town began to write it and then thought better of it. My shop sits at the bend, between a shoe repairer who has been closed for nine years and a grocer who sells three kinds of everything and none of them well. The sign above my door says REPAIRS, and under that, in smaller letters, SOUNDS.

People laugh when they read it. Then they come in.

A sound, when it breaks, does not look broken. That is the trouble. A doorbell that has lost its ring still hangs there, brass and polished, looking perfectly well. A kettle that no longer whistles still boils. You only know it is broken when you press it and hear nothing, or hear something wrong, a thin rattle where there should be a clear note.

I keep them in jars. Not the objects; the sounds themselves. People bring them to me in jam jars, in medicine bottles, once in a thermos flask that still smelled of soup. They do not know how it works. Neither do I, exactly. I know that if you hold the jar to your ear at the right angle you can hear what is inside, the way you hear the sea in a shell. And I know how to coax a sound back into its object, with a tuning fork, a room lined in felt, and a great deal of patience.

I have mended a bicycle bell that rang only in the rain. A music box that played half a lullaby and then stopped, as if it had forgotten the way home. A doorbell that had mislaid its second note. I have mended a kettle's whistle for a woman who said she could not sleep without it, because her husband used to make tea at midnight and the whistle was the last sound she heard before he died.

That one took me a week. The whistle was easy. The sleeping was not my business.

Chapter 2: A Jar of Laughter

The boy came in on a Tuesday in October, when the fog was so thick that Candle Street seemed to end after three houses. He was maybe nine. He carried a jam jar with a label on it, written in careful pencil.

The label said: my mother's laugh.

He set it on the counter and stepped back, as if it might bite.

"Can you fix it?" he said.

I looked at the jar. It was empty, the way all of them are empty. But when I held it up to the light, I could see something inside, faint, like dust turning in a sunbeam.

"Is it broken?" I asked.

"It's getting quieter," he said. "Every week it's quieter. I'm scared that one day I'll hold it up and there'll be nothing."

I put the jar down.

"What's your name?"

"Theo."

"Theo," I said, "how long has it been quiet?"

He looked at his shoes. "Since last winter."

I understood then. I understood the way you understand a cut on your own hand.

"Sit down," I said. "I'll make tea."

He sat on the stool by the window, and I put the kettle on, and for a while neither of us said anything. The fog pressed against the glass. The kettle began, very softly, to sing.

"She laughed at everything," Theo said. "At the dog. At burnt toast. At me, when I got my coat on inside out. She had a laugh like—" He stopped. "I can't do it. I can do everyone else's. I can do my teacher's. I can't do hers."

"You don't have to do it," I said. "You just have to keep it."

"That's why I brought the jar."

I poured the tea. He wrapped both hands around the cup, though it was too hot, and did not drink.

Chapter 3: What Cannot Be Repaired

I worked on that jar for four days.

I did everything I knew. I set it in the felt room and rang a tuning fork beside it, the way I do to wake a stubborn sound. I built a small brass horn, no bigger than my hand, to gather what was inside and pour it into the open air. I tried warmth, and cold, and silence, and once, at two in the morning, I tried talking to it.

Nothing.

The sound was there. I could hear it if I held the jar just so, a low warm murmur, a woman's voice, the beginning of a laugh and then the laugh itself, short and bright, like a coin dropped on stone. But it would not come out. It would not go into anything. It sat in its jar like a bird that has decided not to fly.

On the fifth day, Theo came back. He stood in the doorway with his coat still on, and he did not ask. He just looked at my face, and his own face changed, and I hated that I had caused that.

"It's not broken," I said.

"But it's getting quieter."

"Yes."

"So fix it."

"Theo." I crouched down so I was the same height as him. "A sound doesn't break like a cup breaks. It doesn't get quieter because it's wearing out. It gets quieter because it's being covered up. By other sounds. By time. By the noise of every ordinary day." I took the jar and held it out to him. "I can't put more of it back in. Nobody can. But I can help you hear what's left."

He took the jar and held it to his ear, the way I had taught him. He listened for a long time. Then he nodded, once, and put it in his pocket.

"Thank you," he said, and went out into the fog.

I stood in the shop for a while after he left. Then I went to the back room and opened the cupboard I never open, and took down a jar of my own. The label, in my handwriting, from when I was nineteen, said: Dad, telling the one about the dog.

I had not listened to it in eleven years. I was afraid that if I did, it would be quieter than I remembered, and then I would know for certain that he was really gone.

Chapter 4: The Listening Room

I did not listen to it that night. I put it back.

The next week, Theo came again. He brought the jar, and he brought something else: a small notebook.

"I listened," he said. "I wrote down what I could hear. It's not much. A bit of a giggle. Then a sort of snort. Then her saying my name."

"That's her laugh," I said. "That's exactly it."

"It's not all of it."

"No. But it's the shape of it. And the shape is enough to remember the rest."

He looked at me, and then he did something I did not expect. He laughed. A real laugh, out loud, in my shop, a boy's laugh, sudden and bright and nothing like his mother's, and everything like her.

"There," I said. "That's the rest."

He grinned. Then he looked at the jar in his hand and set it on the counter.

"You can keep it," he said. "I don't need it in a jar anymore."

After he left, I took my father's jar and carried it into the felt room. I sat on the floor, and I held it to my ear, and I listened.

It was faint. It was mostly dust and the low hum of the street and my own breathing. But somewhere in it, small and stubborn, was my father's voice, saying, "So the dog looks at me, right, and he—" and then the beginning of a laugh, cut off, the way it always was, because he could never finish that story without laughing.

I sat there for a long time.

Then I went out and changed the sign on the door. It had said REPAIRS, SOUNDS. Now it says REPAIRS AND LISTENING.

The fog was still there. Candle Street still bent like a comma. But the shop felt bigger, somehow, as if someone had opened a window I had not known was shut.

The next morning, a woman came in with a thermos flask and a question. I put the kettle on.

That is the work, in the end. Not putting things back. Just making sure somebody is there to hear them.
